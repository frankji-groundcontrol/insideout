## PRD quality: what good looks like and how it fails

Across Cagan/SVPG, Amazon's Working Backwards authors, Shreyas Doshi, Lenny Rachitsky, Aakash Gupta, Linear, and Intercom, there is striking convergence on what separates strong PRDs from weak ones: a narrowly-defined customer and a problem stated in the customer's language (never the solution), evidence that the problem is real (verbatim user quotes and data, not invented personas), falsifiable success metrics including counter-metrics, an honest treatment of competition and current alternatives, explicit assumptions with 'what must be true' and 'top 3 reasons this fails' sections, and rank-ordered requirements traceable to measurable objectives with real release criteria. The documented anti-patterns are equally consistent: solution-first requirements, template-complete-but-vacuous content, unvalidated assumptions stated as facts, hand-waved metrics and release criteria, ignoring what customers use today, over- or under-specification, and selling instead of truth-seeking. Critically, every credible source describes PRD quality as the output of a process, not a writing act: a fast, hole-riddled first draft, research to fill named gaps, multiple rounds of cross-functional critique (silent-read reviews, a 7-question reviewer rubric, pre-mortems), prototype validation during — not after — the requirements stage, and a funnel where most ideas are supposed to die. For an agent harness, this implies coaching via interrogation and staged critique rather than one-shot generation, maintaining an assumption ledger and problem-statement anchor, scoring content substance over section coverage, and preferring explicit '[WIP]' gaps over confident filler — the exact failure mode LLM-drafted PRDs share with bad human ones.

- **A strong PRD states the problem to be solved, never the solution. Cagan calls 'what versus how' the central failure mode: PMs bake solutions into requirements either intentionally (strong beliefs) or unintentionally (human nature), which (1) hides the real problem so engineers make wrong assumptions, (2) shuts the door on better solutions, and (3) demoralizes the engineers whose job is solution design. His rule: 'For every requirement in the specification, the product manager should ensure that it is clearly stating the problem to be solved, rather than the solution itself.'**
  - Evidence: Marty Cagan (ex-VP Product at eBay, Netscape, HP; founder of SVPG) in his canonical 'How To Write a Good PRD' paper, 'Common Pitfalls' section — the most-forwarded PRD guide in PM history; I read the full 26-page PDF hosted on svpg.com.
  - Source: [How To Write a Good PRD — Martin Cagan, Silicon Valley Product Group](https://www.svpg.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/How-To-Write-a-Good-PRD.pdf)
  - Harness implication: A PRD-coaching agent should run a per-requirement lint: for each stated requirement, ask 'is this a problem/need or a prescribed solution?' and force the user to restate solution-shaped items as the underlying need (e.g., ten customers asking for different buttons may all be a performance problem). Solution detail belongs only in a clearly-labeled design section.
- **Good PRDs have measurable, prioritized release objectives and explicit release criteria; weak ones hand-wave both. Cagan: the PRD must 'specify exactly what the objectives of this specific product release is, and how they will be measured', rank-order every requirement 1-to-n (not just must/high/nice buckets), hold an 'extremely high bar' for must-have (product literally does not ship if one is missing), map each requirement to the objective it supports (traceability), and spell out minimum release criteria for performance, scalability, reliability, usability, localizability. His completeness test: can an engineer build from it, and can QA write a test plan from it?**
  - Evidence: Same Cagan/SVPG paper, Steps 2, 7, 8, and 9 ('Test Completeness'). He notes release criteria 'are often just hand-waved' and that without rank-ordering, 'other less relevant factors will influence what gets built in what order.'
  - Source: [How To Write a Good PRD — Martin Cagan, Silicon Valley Product Group](https://www.svpg.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/How-To-Write-a-Good-PRD.pdf)
  - Harness implication: The harness should refuse to mark a PRD 'done' until: every objective has a measurement method, every requirement is rank-ordered and traceable to an objective, the must-have list passes a 'would you really hold the release for this?' challenge, and it passes a buildability/testability check (simulate an engineer and a QA reviewer asking 'could I build/test from this?').
- **Detail calibration and unvalidated confidence are documented PRD failure modes. Cagan names 'Too Little Detail' (team members silently assume different things; gaps surface only during build) and 'Too Much Detail' (a spec so massive nobody reads it, obsolete before finished) as twin pitfalls. He calls overconfidence 'one of the biggest and most common mistakes': teams trust their spec, defer validation to beta, and ship first releases 'far from the mark' — prototype-based feasibility, usability, and product-concept testing must happen during the requirements stage. Step 6 tells writers to hunt hidden assumptions: 'Make sure you don't specify a candle in the PRD and prevent yourself from getting a light bulb.'**
  - Evidence: Cagan/SVPG paper, Steps 5-6 and pitfalls 'Too Little Detail', 'Too Much Detail', 'Usability Testing Too Little Too Late', plus 'You Are Not Your Customer' (co-worker enthusiasm predicts nothing; TiVo beat the technically-superior ReplayTV with the mass market).
  - Source: [How To Write a Good PRD — Martin Cagan, Silicon Valley Product Group](https://www.svpg.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/How-To-Write-a-Good-PRD.pdf)
  - Harness implication: The harness needs an assumption-extraction pass (list every implicit assumption, tag each as validated/unvalidated, and demand evidence or a validation plan for unvalidated ones), plus a length/detail governor — flag both underspecified areas (edge cases, error states) and bloated sections nobody will read. It should also flag 'we like it' / internal-dogfooding reasoning as non-evidence.
- **Amazon's PR/FAQ mandates that a spec name a precise customer segment ('If you think your product is for everyone, you are mistaken'), state the problem paragraph from the customer's point of view with TAM justification, and — critically — force the solution paragraph to acknowledge competition: 'Today, customers with this problem use x, y, or z products... Those products fall short of solving x problem(s). Our product addresses these unmet needs in the following ways.' If the solution isn't meaningfully better/cheaper/faster than what customers use today, 'go back to the drawing board.' Discounting current solutions is listed as an explicit common mistake ('there is always a current solution').**
  - Evidence: Official Working Backwards PR/FAQ instructions written by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr (the ex-Amazon executives who authored 'Working Backwards' and ran the process under Bezos), including the Fire Phone as the case study for 'Great Product, Wrong Problem.'
  - Source: [Working Backwards PR/FAQ Instructions & Template — workingbackwards.com (Bryar & Carr)](https://workingbackwards.com/resources/working-backwards-pr-faq/)
  - Harness implication: The harness should hard-require three sections and interrogate each: (1) a narrow named customer segment (reject 'everyone'), (2) the customer's current alternative and why it falls short (the 'why now'/differentiation test), (3) a customer-language problem statement. If the user can't name what customers do today, that's a discovery gap to send them back to, not a blank to fill with plausible text.
- **The PR/FAQ process is explicitly 'truth-seeking vs. selling' and builds structured self-skepticism into the document itself: the internal FAQ must answer 'What assumptions need to be true for this product to be successful?' and 'What are the top three reasons this product will not succeed?', and 'should demonstrate that the authors aren't looking through rose-colored glasses and are in love with their ideas.' Reviewers evaluate against a 7-question rubric: customer clearly defined? problem clearly defined? does the solution address the problem? would customers change behavior? on which dimension is it better/cheaper/faster than substitutes? is TAM/payback big enough? what constraints must be solved?**
  - Evidence: Same official Bryar/Carr instructions — the reviewer question list and the Appendix 2 standard internal FAQs are quoted verbatim on the page; this is the closest thing to a published PRD-quality rubric from a top company.
  - Source: [Working Backwards PR/FAQ Instructions & Template — workingbackwards.com (Bryar & Carr)](https://workingbackwards.com/resources/working-backwards-pr-faq/)
  - Harness implication: Adopt the 7 reviewer questions directly as the harness's evaluation rubric, and require the 'assumptions that must be true' + 'top 3 reasons this will fail' sections as first-class outputs. The agent's reviewer persona should be framed as truth-seeking (improve the idea, possibly kill it) rather than helping the user sell it.
- **Experienced PMs develop specs through many iterative drafts and staged reviews, not one generation pass: at Amazon the first PR/FAQ draft 'should take only a few hours, not a few days'; early drafts deliberately have 'many holes and questions that haven't been answered yet' driving research; then manager + cross-functional peer feedback, then a ~10-person stakeholder review, then executive reviews across multiple drafts. Review meetings start with 15-20 minutes of silent reading and annotation before discussion. The overall process is 'a funnel, not a tunnel' — most PR/FAQs are supposed to die, and AWS took over a year of PR/FAQ iteration before build ('speed vs. velocity').**
  - Evidence: Same Bryar/Carr source, sections 'The process of writing a PR/FAQ', 'The PR/FAQ Review/Meeting', and Appendix 1 'Watch Outs' — first-hand account from the people who ran the process for Bezos.
  - Source: [Working Backwards PR/FAQ Instructions & Template — workingbackwards.com (Bryar & Carr)](https://workingbackwards.com/resources/working-backwards-pr-faq/)
  - Harness implication: Mirror the funnel: the harness should produce a fast, hole-riddled first draft with explicit open questions (not a polished fake-complete doc), then loop draft → targeted questions/research → critique → redraft, escalating rigor per round. It should also support a 'kill this idea' outcome as a legitimate success state, and simulate distinct reviewer roles (peer, finance, legal, exec) across rounds.
- **Pre-mortems are a concrete, teachable process step for stress-testing a plan before commitment: assume the launch has already failed spectacularly, have each participant independently write failure reasons (including impolitic ones), then categorize concerns as Tigers (clear threats), Paper Tigers (ostensible threats you personally discount), and Elephants (the thing nobody is talking about). The output is not the meeting but a prioritized action plan: top 3-5 themes, verbatim threats, mitigation actions, and a named owner per theme — run 1-3 months before launch so there is time to respond.**
  - Evidence: Shreyas Doshi (PM leader at Stripe, Twitter, Google, Yahoo) published his team's actual Stripe template and facilitation script (structured 1-hour agenda: 10-min prompt, 10-min quiet brainstorm, 30-min vote/discussion, 10-min action plan), building on Gary Klein's HBR pre-mortem research.
  - Source: [Pre-mortems: How a Stripe Product Manager prevents problems before launch — Shreyas Doshi](https://coda.io/@shreyas/pre-mortems)
  - Harness implication: The harness should include an explicit pre-mortem phase after a draft stabilizes: 'It's 3 months post-launch and this failed — why?' The agent can generate candidate Tigers/Elephants itself but must also elicit the user's own (especially Elephants — organizationally unspeakable risks an agent can't guess), and convert the top threats into PRD changes or mitigation items with owners, not just a risk list.
- **A PRD can hit every template section and still deserve rejection — 'complete-looking but vacuous' is the dominant modern anti-pattern. Grading a realistic 10-section Airbnb-pricing PRD, product leader Aakash Gupta scores it 2/10 despite full structure, citing: filler content and tautologies ('Ensuring alignment with legal standards'), 'excessively delegates' (no edge cases or user states, dumps design invention on the designer), 'completely lacking in data or analytics' ('comically bad' metrics section), no proof-of-work customer evidence (millions of public complaints existed and none were cited), no mention of the downside metric (conversion hit from showing higher prices), and no competitor survey (Marriott already showed total price). His definition of the modern PRD: shorter but more insightful, includes specific user data, and excites the team to build.**
  - Evidence: Aakash Gupta (product executive, 'Product Growth' newsletter with a large paid PM readership) evaluating a full example PRD line-by-line as he would 'PM homework' — a rare published example of an expert actually scoring a PRD with reasons; article updated April 2026.
  - Source: [Product Requirements Documents (PRDs): A Modern Guide — Aakash Gupta, Product Growth](https://www.news.aakashg.com/p/product-requirements-documents-prds)
  - Harness implication: Template compliance is a near-worthless signal; the harness's rubric must score content substance per section: does the metrics section name specific metrics with baselines and expected deltas (including counter-metrics/downsides)? Is there cited real user evidence? Are edge cases and user states enumerated? A section filled with generic prose should score lower than an honest '@Legal to confirm' or '[WIP]' placeholder — the agent should prefer explicit gaps over confident filler, which is also the exact failure mode of LLM-generated PRDs.
- **Lenny Rachitsky's widely-used one-page PRD template is problem-first and evidence-demanding: Description (what is it), Problem (what problem is this solving), 'Why: How do we know this is a real problem and worth solving?', Success ('How do we know if we've solved this problem?'), Audience, and What. The prescribed process is iterative and social: share the draft with the whole team, ask for feedback, integrate, re-share, repeat — then keep returning to the problem statement throughout execution (every design review starts by reviewing the problem statement; before finalizing designs ask 'Am I confident this solves the problem we set out to solve?').**
  - Evidence: Official Atlassian/Confluence template page authored with Lenny Rachitsky (ex-Airbnb PM lead, author of the most-read PM newsletter), summarizing his 'Three-Step Framework for Solving Problems.' The 'Why/how do we know' question is the template's built-in evidence gate.
  - Source: [Lenny's Product Requirements template — Atlassian Confluence (Lenny Rachitsky)](https://www.atlassian.com/software/confluence/templates/lennys-product-requirements)
  - Harness implication: Use this as the minimal skeleton for a coaching harness: six questions, of which 'How do we know this is a real problem?' and 'How do we know if we've solved it?' are the two the agent should press hardest on. The harness should also persist the problem statement as an anchor artifact and re-surface it at later checkpoints ('does the current solution still solve the original problem?') rather than treating the PRD as write-once.
- **Linear's public method declares user stories an anti-pattern ('At Linear, we don't write user stories and think they're an anti-pattern in product development') and prescribes quoting user feedback verbatim instead of summarizing: 'a customer describes the pain point more authentically than you could summarize it', with a link back to the source conversation. It also holds that writing your own spec is the thinking: 'When you write your own issues, it forces you to think through the problem at a deep level', and that the project owner 'writes specs and gathers feedback until we feel like we have the right approach' — UX debated at product/feature level, not smeared across task-level story boilerplate.**
  - Evidence: Linear Method (the company's published product-development handbook, widely adopted by startups), 'Write issues not user stories' chapter — read in full.
  - Source: [Write issues not user stories — Linear Method](https://linear.app/method/write-issues-not-user-stories)
  - Harness implication: Two direct rules for the harness: (1) prefer verbatim user quotes with source links over paraphrased 'users want X' claims — the agent should ask 'what did the user actually say?' and paste it; (2) the agent must not write the PRD for the user — because writing is the thinking, the harness should coach via questions and critique, generating scaffolding and challenges rather than finished prose the user rubber-stamps.
- **Intercom's job-stories critique explains why invented personas make PRDs weak: 'Personas are imaginary customers defined by attributes that don't acknowledge causality' — demographics don't explain why anyone uses or ignores a product. User stories additionally 'couple implementation with motivations and outcomes' (so when a feature fails you can't tell whether the implementation or the motivation assumption was wrong) and 'ignore context, situations, and anxieties.' The prescribed alternative is grounded in observation: start with the high-level job, observe how people solve the problem now, then write job stories capturing situation ('when...'), motivation, anxiety, and desired outcome, and only then design a solution whose every element traces to a job.**
  - Evidence: Alan Klement's canonical guest post on the Intercom blog (Intercom later published a whole book, 'Intercom on Jobs-to-be-Done', building on it), with a worked example of designing a profile view from job stories for a car-loan product.
  - Source: [Designing Features Using Job Stories — Alan Klement, Inside Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/using-job-stories-design-features-ui-ux/)
  - Harness implication: The harness should treat demographic personas ('Sarah, 34, marketing manager') as a smell and steer toward causal framing: for each target user, demand the situation, motivation, anxiety, and current workaround, sourced from observed behavior. It should also keep motivation claims decoupled from the proposed implementation so each can be separately validated — useful structure for the assumption ledger the agent maintains.

## Agent-harness engineering guidance

Anthropic's engineering guidance converges on a clear hierarchy: use the simplest thing that works — a single LLM call with retrieval, then a fixed workflow (prompt chain, routing, evaluator-optimizer), and only an autonomous agent loop when steps can't be predicted; multi-agent orchestrator-worker architectures pay off only for parallelizable, breadth-first research-style work and cost ~15x chat tokens, while write/generation tasks with shared context (like producing one coherent document) are explicitly called a bad fit — Cognition goes further and says parallel subagents making implicit conflicting decisions is the primary failure mode. For fidelity, the documented playbook is: gates/checklists between fixed pipeline steps, quote-extraction and cite-then-retract verification before writing claims, permission to say "I don't know", structured rules-based checks over fuzzy LLM judges, and human checkpoints at high-risk or repeated-failure moments. Critique loops (evaluator-optimizer) are only recommended when evaluation criteria are clear and articulated feedback demonstrably improves output — otherwise they thrash; a single rubric-based judge call scoring 0.0-1.0 with pass/fail proved more consistent than multiple judges. For long multi-turn sessions, context is a finite "attention budget": compaction, structured note-taking to external memory, and writing artifacts to a filesystem (passing lightweight references, not full text, between steps) prevent context rot and the "game of telephone." For a PRD-coaching harness this all points to one single-threaded coaching agent over a fixed workflow skeleton, with the PRD living as a persistent artifact, a rubric-based critique gate, and the human as the loop's ground truth.

- **Default to the simplest architecture: a single augmented LLM call, then a fixed workflow, then an agent — add complexity only when it demonstrably improves measured outcomes. Workflows (predefined code paths) win for predictability and consistency on well-defined tasks; agents (LLM-directed loops) win only when the required steps can't be predicted and you can trust the model's decision-making, at the price of higher cost and compounding errors.**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's Applied AI team, distilling 'dozens of teams building LLM agents across industries': 'the most successful implementations weren't using complex frameworks... they were building with simple, composable patterns'; 'workflows offer predictability and consistency for well-defined tasks, whereas agents are the better option when flexibility and model-driven decision-making are needed'; 'The autonomous nature of agents means higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors.'
  - Source: [Building effective agents (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
  - Harness implication: A PRD-coaching session has a largely predictable macro-shape (discover context → clarify problem → draft sections → critique → revise), so the harness should be a fixed workflow skeleton with gated stages, using agentic freedom only inside stages (e.g., which clarifying questions to ask), not an open-ended autonomous loop over the whole session.
- **Multi-agent orchestrator-worker systems pay off only for high-value, breadth-first, parallelizable tasks — they use ~15x the tokens of chat and ~4x the tokens of single-agent interactions, and Anthropic explicitly says domains where agents must share context or have many inter-dependencies (e.g., most coding/generation work) 'are not a good fit for multi-agent systems today.'**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's production Research team reports measured data: 'agents typically use about 4× more tokens than chat interactions, and multi-agent systems use about 15× more tokens than chats'; multi-agent beat single-agent Opus by 90.2% but only on breadth-first research evals where token spend explains 80% of performance variance; 'some domains that require all agents to share the same context or involve many dependencies between agents are not a good fit.'
  - Source: [How we built our multi-agent research system (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
  - Harness implication: Writing one coherent PRD is exactly the shared-context, high-dependency case Anthropic warns against: do not fan out PRD sections to parallel writer subagents. If subagents are used at all, restrict them to read-only breadth tasks (competitor scans, prior-doc retrieval) that return condensed summaries to one writing agent.
- **Parallel subagents doing generation fragment implicit decisions and produce incoherent output; the reliable default is a single-threaded linear agent where every action sees the full trace. Cognition's two principles — 'share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages' and 'actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results' — should by default rule out architectures that violate them.**
  - Evidence: Cognition (builders of Devin, a production long-running coding agent) illustrate with the Flappy Bird example: two subagents given well-specified subtasks still produced stylistically conflicting components because 'the actions subagent 1 took and the actions subagent 2 took were based on conflicting assumptions not prescribed upfront'; they note even Claude Code's subagents only answer questions, never write code in parallel, precisely to avoid this.
  - Source: [Don't Build Multi-Agents (Cognition)](https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)
  - Harness implication: One coaching agent must own the PRD end-to-end so tone, scope decisions, and terminology stay consistent; any helper (researcher, critic) must receive the full conversation/decision trace, not a paraphrased subtask, and must never edit the document directly.
- **The evaluator-optimizer (generator + critic loop) pattern converges only under two conditions: evaluation criteria are clear, and LLM output demonstrably improves when feedback is articulated — with the critic able to produce that feedback. Absent these, iterative refinement adds latency without measurable value (thrash).**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's pattern catalog states the workflow 'is particularly effective when we have clear evaluation criteria, and when iterative refinement provides measurable value. The two signs of good fit are, first, that LLM responses can be demonstrably improved when a human articulates their feedback; and second, that the LLM can provide such feedback' — with document writing/refinement given as the canonical example.
  - Source: [Building effective agents (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)
  - Harness implication: A PRD critique loop qualifies (PRD quality criteria are articulable: measurable success metrics, testable requirements, named non-goals), but the harness must encode those criteria as an explicit rubric the critic scores against, cap iterations (e.g., 1-2 passes), and stop when scores plateau rather than looping on vibes.
- **For LLM-as-judge evaluation, a single judge call with one rubric-based prompt outputting a 0.0-1.0 score per criterion plus an overall pass/fail was more consistent and better aligned with human judgment than an ensemble of specialized judges; rubric axes used in production were factual accuracy, citation accuracy, completeness, source quality, and tool efficiency — and human spot-checking still caught failure modes the judge missed.**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's Research-feature team: 'We experimented with multiple judges to evaluate each component, but found that a single LLM call with a single prompt outputting scores from 0.0-1.0 and a pass-fail grade was the most consistent and aligned with human judgements'; human testers separately caught agents preferring SEO content farms over authoritative sources, fixed via source-quality heuristics in prompts.
  - Source: [How we built our multi-agent research system (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
  - Harness implication: Design the PRD critic as ONE rubric-scoring call (per-dimension 0-1 scores + pass/fail), not a panel of specialist critics; keep the rubric dimensions few and concrete (grounded-in-user-input, completeness vs. template, testability of requirements), and periodically human-review critic verdicts to recalibrate the rubric.
- **Anthropic's documented anti-fabrication playbook for high-fidelity generation: explicitly permit 'I don't know'; extract word-for-word quotes before reasoning over long documents; make every claim cite a supporting quote and retract claims that can't be supported; restrict the model to provided material ('external knowledge restriction'); and use chain-of-thought or best-of-N comparison to surface inconsistencies.**
  - Evidence: Official Claude platform guidance (Reduce hallucinations): 'Explicitly give Claude permission to admit uncertainty. This simple technique can drastically reduce false information'; 'have Claude verify each claim by finding a supporting quote after it generates a response. If it can't find a quote, it must retract the claim'; 'Explicitly instruct Claude to only use information from provided documents and not its general knowledge.'
  - Source: [Reduce hallucinations (Claude Platform Docs)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)
  - Harness implication: The coach must never invent user requirements: harness rules should force every PRD claim (user need, metric, constraint) to trace to something the user actually said or a fetched source, mark unverified items as [ASSUMPTION — confirm], and prefer asking the user over filling gaps — a verify-before-write pass that retracts or flags unsupported statements before the draft is shown.
- **The core reliability loop is gather context → take action → verify work → repeat, and the best verification is explicit rules-based checks ('clearly defined rules for an output, then explaining which rules failed and why'); LLM-as-judge on fuzzy criteria is 'generally not a very robust method' with heavy latency tradeoffs, to be used only when any performance boost justifies the cost.**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's Claude Agent SDK post (the harness that powers Claude Code, used internally for 'almost all of our major agent loops') ranks verification approaches: defined rules first (linting analogy — 'the more in-depth in feedback the better'), visual feedback second, LLM-as-judge last with the explicit robustness caveat.
  - Source: [Building agents with the Claude Agent SDK (Anthropic)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-agents-with-the-claude-agent-sdk)
  - Harness implication: Encode as much PRD quality checking as possible in deterministic validators the harness runs on the draft (required sections present, every requirement has an acceptance criterion, metrics are quantified, no TBDs in shipped sections) and feed failed rules back to the agent; reserve the LLM critic only for genuinely fuzzy qualities like clarity and internal consistency.
- **Context is a finite 'attention budget' subject to context rot; for long multi-turn sessions the three documented levers are compaction (summarize-and-restart tuned for recall first, then precision — tool-result clearing being the safest form), structured note-taking to external memory that gets re-read after resets, and subagents with clean contexts that return 1,000-2,000-token distilled summaries; system prompts should sit at the 'right altitude' (heuristics, not brittle hardcoded if-else logic) with a curated minimal tool set and canonical few-shot examples instead of a laundry list of edge cases.**
  - Evidence: Anthropic Applied AI team: 'as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases' (citing Chroma's context-rot research); Claude Code's compaction 'preserves architectural decisions, unresolved bugs, and implementation details while discarding redundant tool outputs'; Claude-plays-Pokémon shows note-taking sustaining coherent multi-hour behavior across context resets.
  - Source: [Effective context engineering for AI agents (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)
  - Harness implication: PRD coaching sessions span many turns: keep the evolving PRD and a decision log (choices made, open questions, user preferences) as external files the agent re-reads rather than relying on chat history; compact conversation aggressively but never compact away recorded user decisions; keep the coach's system prompt as heuristics-at-altitude plus a few canonical PRD examples.
- **Have subagents write outputs to a filesystem and pass lightweight references — not full text — through the coordinator, to avoid the 'game of telephone' where relaying work through an orchestrator degrades fidelity; also, orchestrators must give subagents an objective, output format, tool/source guidance, and explicit task boundaries, plus effort-scaling rules (e.g., simple fact-finding = 1 agent, 3-10 tool calls), or agents duplicate work and over-invest.**
  - Evidence: Anthropic's multi-agent appendix: 'Subagent output to a filesystem to minimize the game of telephone... prevents information loss during multi-stage processing and reduces token overhead from copying large outputs through conversation history'; early lead agents that gave vague instructions ('research the semiconductor shortage') caused subagents to duplicate or misinterpret work, and agents 'spawning 50 subagents for simple queries' until explicit effort-scaling rules were prompted in.
  - Source: [How we built our multi-agent research system (Anthropic Engineering)](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
  - Harness implication: In the harness, every stage (interviewer, drafter, critic) should read/write the PRD artifact and decision log directly on disk and exchange references, never paste the whole document through orchestration messages; any delegated step needs a fully specified brief (objective, output format, boundaries) and an explicit effort budget.
- **OpenAI's deployment guidance mirrors Anthropic's: maximize a single agent's capabilities first (adding tools incrementally, using prompt templates with variables before splitting), split into multiple agents only on concrete failure signals (prompts with many conditional branches, or overlapping/ambiguous tools — some systems handle 15+ distinct tools fine while others fail with 10 overlapping ones); layer guardrails (relevance/safety classifiers, rules-based checks, output validation) and hard-wire human intervention on two triggers: exceeding failure thresholds and high-risk/irreversible actions.**
  - Evidence: OpenAI's practical guide, distilled from customer deployments: 'Our general recommendation is to maximize a single agent's capabilities first. More agents can provide intuitive separation of concepts, but can introduce additional complexity and overhead'; 'Two primary triggers typically warrant human intervention: Exceeding failure thresholds... High-risk actions... should trigger human oversight until confidence in the agent's reliability grows.'
  - Source: [A practical guide to building agents (OpenAI)](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)
  - Harness implication: Start the PRD coach as one agent with a small distinct toolset (read/write PRD, log decision, ask user, run rubric check) and a templated stage prompt; only split into separate agents if the single prompt provably fails; treat 'finalize/export PRD' and 'overwrite a user-approved section' as high-risk actions requiring an explicit human approval gate, and escalate to the user after N failed critique iterations instead of looping.

## Academic evidence: elicitation, self-critique, and multi-agent writing

The academic literature converges on four hard lessons for an interactive writing/coaching harness. (1) Question asking is the highest-leverage stage: STORM's perspective-guided questioning nearly triples the unique sources gathered before writing (99.8 vs 39.6 references) and Wikipedia editors rate the resulting articles more organized (+25 points) — and GATE's preregistered human study shows LM-led interviewing beats asking users to write the spec themselves at lower perceived effort, which is the core premise of a PRD coach. (2) Self-critique only pays off with an external grounding signal or on open-ended quality dimensions: Self-Refine gains up to +49 points on preference tasks but ~0 on math; intrinsic self-correction actually lowers accuracy (GPT-3.5 CommonSenseQA 75.8→38.1); Reflexion and CRITIC show the same loop works once wired to unit tests or tools. (3) Multi-agent debate delivers real gains (GSM8K 77→85) but does not reliably beat equal-compute self-consistency, and its dominant failure mode is mis-calibrated agreement — a forced-disagreement "devil" critic drove accuracy below the first draft. (4) For evaluation, decomposed binary checklists (CheckEval) raise inter-judge agreement from ~0.05 to 0.67 (human-level) versus Likert rubrics, so a PRD coach's quality gates should be binary checklist items, not 1-5 scores. Related verified work (Zhang et al., arXiv:2410.13788) adds that clarifying questions should be trained/judged by their downstream effect on the final answer (+4-5% F1 from "double-turn" preference labeling), not by immediate plausibility.

- **STORM's perspective-guided question asking works primarily by widening pre-writing research coverage: it collects 99.83 unique references per topic vs 54.36 without perspectives and 39.56 without simulated multi-turn conversation, and experienced Wikipedia editors rated STORM articles well-organized 70% of the time vs 45% for the outline-driven RAG baseline (+25 points, p=0.005), preferring STORM 26-14. Removing the outline stage entirely collapses article entity recall from 14.10 to 7.39. Notably, verifiability did NOT improve (3.80 vs 3.85).**
  - Evidence: NAACL 2024 paper from Stanford OVAL (Shao, Jiang, Khattab, Lam). Numbers verified directly from the paper's Tables 2, 3, 5, and 6 (ar5iv full text) and the arXiv abstract: outline heading soft recall GPT-4 92.73 (STORM) vs 92.39 (w/o perspective) vs 88.75 (w/o conversation) vs 89.55 (oRAG); human eval on 20 article pairs by two Wikipedia editors each.
  - Source: [Assisting in Writing Wikipedia-like Articles From Scratch with Large Language Models (STORM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)
  - Harness implication: Structure the PRD coach's pre-writing phase as multi-perspective question generation (distinct stakeholder personas each interrogating the topic across multiple turns): the measured payoff is breadth of gathered material and document organization — and the outline stage is the single biggest lever on final quality. Do not expect question diversity to fix factual verifiability; that needs separate grounding.
- **Co-STORM shows that having agents ask questions on the user's behalf — especially a 'moderator' that raises questions from collected-but-unused information — measurably surfaces unknown unknowns: report Depth 3.77 and Novelty 3.05 vs 3.43/2.50 for STORM+QA and 3.26/2.44 for a RAG chatbot (p<0.05), with 6.04 unique cited URLs per QA turn vs ~2.9. Removing the moderator hurts more than removing multiple experts (Depth drops 3.77→3.41). In human eval, 70% preferred it over Google Search and 78% over a RAG chatbot; 89% of sampled moderator utterances were rated as steering discourse in a new, interesting direction.**
  - Evidence: EMNLP 2024 paper (Jiang, Shao, Lam, Stanford). Numbers verified from Tables 3-4 and Section 6 of the full text: automatic eval on the WildSeek dataset with simulated users plus an IRB-approved 20-participant study with counterbalanced pairwise comparisons.
  - Source: [Into the Unknown Unknowns: Engaged Human Learning through Participation in Language Model Agent Conversations (Co-STORM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15232)
  - Harness implication: A PRD coach should not put the entire questioning burden on the user or on a single interviewer agent. Include a moderator-style component that tracks what has been gathered but not yet discussed and injects questions from that pool — the measured effect is deeper, more novel coverage with less user mental effort, exactly the 'requirements the user didn't think to mention' problem.
- **Self-Refine's gains are strongly task-dependent: iterative self-feedback lifts open-ended, preference-judged generation dramatically (GPT-4 dialogue response preference 25.4→74.6, +49.2; sentiment rewriting 3.8→36.2) but is ~useless on reasoning (GSM8K +0.2 for GPT-4/ChatGPT; +0 for GPT-3.5, rising to +4.8 only with oracle correctness feedback). The active ingredient is specific, actionable feedback: on sentiment reversal, scores fall 43.2 → 31.2 (generic feedback) → 0 (no feedback), and most of the gain arrives in the first 1-2 of 4 iterations with diminishing returns after.**
  - Evidence: NeurIPS 2023 paper (Madaan et al.), verified from Tables 1, 2, 9 and Figure 4 of the full text; results replicated across GPT-3.5, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 with Wilson confidence intervals (nearly all GPT-4 gains statistically significant).
  - Source: [Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651)
  - Harness implication: Self-critique loops are worth building for exactly the PRD case — open-ended writing quality (clarity, tone, completeness of sections) is where they measurably work. But the critique prompt must demand specific, actionable, per-dimension feedback with scores (generic 'improve this' loses most of the gain), and the loop should cap at 2-3 iterations.
- **Intrinsic self-correction (no external signal) measurably degrades reasoning: after two self-correction rounds, GPT-3.5 fell 75.8→41.8 on CommonSenseQA and GPT-4 fell 95.5→89.0 on GSM8K; Llama-2-70b fell 62.0→36.5. Models flip correct answers to incorrect more often than the reverse; earlier reported gains (RCI, Reflexion on reasoning) came from using oracle ground-truth labels to decide when to stop. The same paper shows multi-agent debate does not outperform self-consistency at an equal number of model responses.**
  - Evidence: ICLR 2024 paper from Google DeepMind (Huang, Chen, Zhou et al.), verified from Tables 2-6 and Section 4 of the full text; tested across multiple feedback-prompt variants, so the failure is not prompt-specific.
  - Source: [Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)
  - Harness implication: Never gate the coach's revision loop on the model's own judgment of whether content is 'correct' — a bare 'review your answer' prompt is a biasing input that flips good content to bad. Any correctness-flavored check needs an external anchor: the user's confirmation, the interview transcript, or a verifiable checklist. Also benchmark any debate feature against simply sampling N drafts and voting.
- **Reflexion shows verbal self-reflection works when it amplifies an external outcome signal: with self-generated unit tests as ground truth, HumanEval Python pass@1 rose 80.1→91.0 and AlfWorld task completion improved 22 points over ReAct. The ablation is decisive: on the 50 hardest HumanEval-Rust problems, self-reflection WITHOUT test execution scored 0.52 — below the 0.60 no-reflection baseline — while the full loop scored 0.68. When the self-generated tests were flaky (MBPP, 16.3% false-positive rate), Reflexion underperformed the baseline (77.1 vs 80.1).**
  - Evidence: NeurIPS 2023 paper (Shinn et al.), verified from Tables 1-3 and Sections 4.1-4.3 of the full text, including the false-positive-rate analysis that explains the MBPP regression.
  - Source: [Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)
  - Harness implication: Reflection memory ('what went wrong last draft, what to change') is valuable in a coaching harness, but only downstream of a grounded evaluator. The PRD analog of unit tests is a checklist verified against the user's actual answers; if that evaluator is noisy, the reflection loop can push output BELOW the single-pass baseline, so evaluator precision matters more than reflection prompt quality.
- **CRITIC quantifies that tools, not introspection, make critique loops work: tool-interactive critiquing gave ChatGPT +7.7 F1 on QA, +7.0% absolute on math, and a 79.2% reduction in toxicity probability, while the same loop without tools contributed between -0.03 and +2.33 F1 (sometimes below the initial output) and dropped math performance 1.8 points on text-davinci-003. 2-3 correction rounds capture most of the benefit; the authors attribute the failure of pure self-critique to LLMs being unable to identify what they know (self-verification unreliability).**
  - Evidence: ICLR 2024 paper (Gou et al., Tsinghua/Microsoft), verified from the abstract, Tables 1-3, and Sections 4.2-4.4 of the full text, including explicit 'CRITIC w/o Tool' ablations across ChatGPT, text-davinci-003, and LLaMA-2 7B/13B/70B.
  - Source: [CRITIC: Large Language Models Can Self-Correct with Tool-Interactive Critiquing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738)
  - Harness implication: Wire every critique in the harness to something checkable: retrieval over the interview transcript, the product's source documents, or programmatic checks (section presence, metric definitions, testable acceptance criteria). A critic persona with no external evidence adds roughly nothing and can subtract; budget ~2-3 tool-grounded critique rounds.
- **The canonical multi-agent debate result (Du et al.): 3 ChatGPT agents debating for 2 rounds raised GSM8K 77.0→85.0, arithmetic 67.0→81.8, MMLU 63.9→71.1, biography factuality 66.0→73.8, and chess-move validity 29.3→45.2 versus a single agent — beating both single-agent reflection (which HURT on MMLU: 63.9→57.7) and simple majority voting (69.0 on arithmetic). Gains rise monotonically with more agents and rounds; uncertain facts tend to get disagreed away and omitted, which reduces hallucination in the consensus.**
  - Evidence: Du, Li, Torralba, Tenenbaum, Mordatch (MIT/Google), verified from Tables 1-2 and Sections 2-3 of the full text; identical prompts across tasks, black-box access only. (Caveat from Huang et al. 2310.01798: at matched total responses, this method did not beat self-consistency on GSM8K.)
  - Source: [Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325)
  - Harness implication: Debate's honest, replicated benefit for a writing harness is hallucination pruning — facts agents disagree on get dropped from the consensus — which maps well to reconciling multiple draft PRD sections. But treat 'debate beats single model' claims with the equal-compute caveat: N parallel drafts + selection is the fair baseline to beat.
- **A systematic benchmark of MAD strategies found multi-agent debate does not reliably outperform self-consistency or Medprompt across 7 datasets, and identified agent agreement level as the decisive failure axis: Multi-Persona's forced-disagreement 'devil' agent reduced accuracy BELOW the first agent's initial answer (sycophantic over-correction of correct drafts), while prompting agents with an explicit agreement intensity ('agree with the other agents X% of the time') flipped Multi-Persona from worst-performing to best, surpassing all non-debate protocols. The optimal direction is task-dependent: high agreement helped MedQA/PubMedQA; strong disagreement helped counter-intuitive CIAR questions.**
  - Evidence: Smit et al. (InstaDeep), ICML 2024, verified from the abstract, Table 2, Figures 3-6, and the agreement-modulation section of the full text; large-scale benchmark over MedQA, PubMedQA, MMLU, CosmosQA, CIAR, GPQA, and chess with K-fold hyperparameter analysis.
  - Source: [Should we be going MAD? A Look at Multi-Agent Debate Strategies for LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)
  - Harness implication: If the PRD coach uses multiple critic personas, calibrate how contrarian they are as an explicit, tunable parameter — a naively adversarial critic measurably corrupts good drafts, and the right agreement level differs by task type (routine template sections want agreeable critics; assumption-busting sections want disagreeable ones). Ship the knob, not a fixed 'devil's advocate'.
- **GATE's preregistered human study (388 participants, 3 domains) shows LM-driven interactive elicitation beats asking users to specify the task themselves: LM elicitation matched or exceeded user-written prompts in 6/10 settings on absolute accuracy and 7/10 on time-normalized AUC, with generative yes/no questions beating user-written prompts in every setting studied; in content recommendation it also significantly beat supervised learning and pool-based active learning. Users rated the interactive interview as equally or less mentally demanding than writing the specification, and elicitation did not measurably shift users' underlying preferences.**
  - Evidence: Li, Tamkin, Goodman, Andreas (MIT/Stanford/Anthropic), verified from the abstract, Sections 4-5, and Figures 3-4 of the full text; preregistered at OSF, 20-30 participants per domain-method pair, 5-minute interaction budget per user.
  - Source: [Eliciting Human Preferences with Language Models (GATE)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11589)
  - Harness implication: This is the strongest direct evidence for the PRD-coach premise itself: interviewing the user outperforms making the user write the spec, at lower perceived effort. Design detail that transfers: easy-to-answer formats (yes/no questions, concrete edge cases to accept/reject) were the most consistently winning elicitation moves — favor them over broad open-ended questions, especially early. (Follow-up STaR-GATE, arXiv:2403.19154, shows a questioner fine-tuned on its own best interviews reaches 72% win rate over its base model after two iterations.)
- **CheckEval shows that decomposing evaluation into binary checklist questions fixes LLM-judge unreliability: across 12 evaluator models, checklist scoring raised inter-evaluator agreement by +0.45 on average versus Likert-based G-Eval/SEEval — e.g., among large models on SummEval, Krippendorff's alpha rose from 0.05 (G-Eval) to 0.67, comparable to human inter-rater agreement (kappa ~0.7) — while maintaining or improving correlation with human judgments (best rho=0.55 SummEval, r=0.65 Topical-Chat) and reducing score variance and evaluator-choice sensitivity.**
  - Evidence: Lee et al. (Boston University/SNU/KAIST/NAVER), verified from the abstract, Tables 2-4, and Sections 5-6 of the full text; includes a human validation where three annotators applying the same checklist (~10k binary judgments) agreed with each other (kappa 0.53) and with LLM judges (kappa 0.49).
  - Source: [CheckEval: A reliable LLM-as-a-Judge framework for evaluating text generation using checklists](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18771)
  - Harness implication: The PRD coach's quality gates and rubrics should be decomposed binary questions ('Does every user story have acceptance criteria? yes/no'), not 1-5 holistic scores: binary checklists make the judge model-agnostic and reproducible (agreement jumps from near-zero to human-level) and make failures traceable to specific criteria the coach can then target in the next revision loop.

## Existing PRD-AI products

The AI-PRD tool space has converged on a clear lesson: one-shot generation from a short prompt produces "invisible" generic documents (independent graders scored bare LLM PRDs C to F), while the tools users actually praise all add an elicitation layer — a persistent user/company profile, a built-in template, and a mandatory 3-5 clarifying-question round before drafting (ChatPRD's core loop). The dominant complaint across reviews and comparison posts is generic/hallucinated output that needs "substantial rewriting," and the market's two answers are (a) grounding PRDs in real customer evidence with citations (BuildBetter's evidence-linked PRDs) and (b) refusing to invent: open-source PRD agents gate each section behind explicit checks — per-requirement data-source/rule/exception validation, traffic-light completeness scoring before generation, and clarification loops that run until a confidence threshold. Pricing signals show buyers pay for saved context ("projects with saved knowledge"), templates, and export/integrations rather than raw generation quality, since frontier models match or beat dedicated tools on prose. For a PRD-coaching harness, the strongest patterns to copy are: question-first with a small bounded question budget, score-gated generation, per-section "ask, don't invent" rules, rubric-based post-generation review with one-click refine actions, and persistent context so users never re-explain their product.
